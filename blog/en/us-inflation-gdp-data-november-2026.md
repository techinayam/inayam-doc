Here's a question that sounds silly but isn't. If the American economy just grew at 3.4%, which is genuinely strong, why is everyone in markets acting like something went wrong?

Because prices are still rising too fast. And when a hot economy meets stubborn inflation, the Federal Reserve does the one thing it's built to do. It raises interest rates.

For this one I went through the actual releases from the BLS, the BEA and the Fed, not the headlines written about them. ## First, inflation. October came in hot

The Consumer Price Index for October came out on 10 November. Prices rose 0.4% in the month, down from 0.6% in September. Technically that's a slowdown. But keep 0.4% a month going for a year and you're looking at nearly 5% inflation, so nobody at the Fed is relaxing.

The number that really bothered people was **core inflation**, which strips out food and fuel. It rose 0.3%, when economists had pencilled in about 0.2%.

A 0.1 gap sounds like nothing. It isn't. Food and fuel jump around for all sorts of reasons: a bad harvest, an OPEC meeting, a cold winter. Core is everything that's supposed to stay calm, like rent, insurance, a haircut or a car service. When core beats the forecast, it tells the Fed that inflation has moved in and unpacked its bags.

### Wait, where's the yearly inflation number?

If you searched "US inflation rate October 2026" and got a confusing answer, it's not you.

During the 2025 US government shutdown, the BLS couldn't collect prices for October 2025. No base month means no year-on-year comparison, so for October 2026 the BLS simply doesn't publish an official 12-month rate for the affected indexes.

The last annual figure is September's 3.7%. A few independent trackers have plugged the gap themselves and arrived at roughly 4.0% for October. That's fine as a rough guide, but it's their estimate, not the government's. I'd be a little suspicious of any article that quotes it as official.

## Now growth. And 3.4% is a big number

The BEA's first estimate for July to September came out on 29 October.

> **What it says:** "At a quarterly rate, the third-quarter increase was 0.8%."
> **What it means:** The US economy didn't grow 3.4% in three months. It grew 0.8%. The 3.4% is what you'd get if that pace held for a whole year. America reports GDP this "annualized" way. India reports quarterly growth against the same quarter last year, so you can't put the two numbers side by side and compare.

What pushed growth up? Businesses investing, people spending, and the government spending. Exports fell, and imports grew faster than the quarter before, which took some of the shine off. Nominal GDP grew 5.6%, taking the economy to about $33.01 trillion a year.

One caveat I'd want if I were reading this: it's only the *advance* estimate, the first of three. By the BEA's own track record, the figure has moved by about half a percentage point on average by the third estimate since 2002, and about 0.3 since 2021. The second estimate comes out on 25 November, so 3.4% could easily become 3.1 or 3.7.

***

## So why does good growth lead to rate hikes?

Think of a pressure cooker on high flame. Growth is the flame, inflation is the pressure building inside, and the Fed's interest rate is the only knob it can turn. To cool things down, it only turns one way.

Here's what the Fed said on 28 October:

> **What it says:** "The Committee decided to maintain the target range for the federal funds rate at 3-3/4 to 4 percent."
> **What it means:** No change, for now. But the vote was 10 to 2. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack and Governor Lisa Cook both wanted a 0.25% hike right then. The minutes released later showed that even a few members who voted to hold called it a "close call."

That's a committee one bad data release away from moving. Then the data came. October's jobs report beat expectations, with 165,000 new payrolls reported, and then the CPI above. By mid-November, odds of a 25 basis point hike on 9 December were being quoted between 85% and 96%, depending on the day you checked. (A basis point is one-hundredth of a percent, so 25 bp is 0.25%.)

Bond markets moved first, as they usually do. The US 10-year yield was around 4.90% on 24 November after getting close to 5% earlier in the month, and the dollar index ended the week of 20 November up 0.3%.

## My take

The US economy has been stronger this year than almost anyone expected, and right now that's the problem. Growth this good keeps demand high, demand keeps prices rising, and a Fed that already had two members voting for a hike in October doesn't need much more convincing.

If I had to pick one number to watch, it's tomorrow's PCE. Most people know CPI, but the Fed officially measures its 2% target with the PCE price index. If PCE tells the same story CPI did, I honestly don't see how the Fed avoids a hike in December.

**What would change my mind:** if October core PCE comes in at 0.2% or lower *and* the November jobs report on 4 December is clearly weak, a December pause comes back into play. One of the two on its own probably isn't enough.

## FAQ

**What was the US inflation rate in October 2026?**
Consumer prices rose 0.4% in October on a monthly basis, and core prices rose 0.3%. The BLS didn't publish an official 12-month rate for October because October 2025 data was never collected during the 2025 government shutdown. The last annual figure was 3.7% for September.

**How fast did the US economy grow in Q3 2026?**
Real GDP grew at a 3.4% annualized rate in July to September, up from 2.2% in Q2, according to the BEA's advance estimate. That's about 0.8% growth within the quarter itself. The second estimate is due on 25 November 2026.

**Will the Fed raise interest rates in December 2026?**
Markets think it's very likely. Reports based on CME FedWatch put the odds of a 0.25% hike on 9 December at roughly 85% to 96% in mid-November. October PCE inflation (25 November) and the November jobs report (4 December) are the last big data points before the decision.

**What is the difference between CPI and PCE?**
Both measure inflation. CPI tracks prices paid by urban consumers and comes from the BLS. PCE is broader, comes from the BEA, and is the measure the Fed uses for its official 2% inflation target.

**Does a US rate hike affect Indian home loan EMIs?**
Not directly. Indian loan rates follow the RBI. But higher US rates can pull foreign money out of India and weaken the rupee, which can limit how much the RBI can cut rates.
