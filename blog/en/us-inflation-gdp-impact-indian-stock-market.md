Something about November didn't add up, at least at first.

The US put out data that basically told the world "interest rates are going up again." The usual script says foreign money pulls out of India when that happens. Instead, foreign investors bought Indian shares at a pace we hadn't seen in a long time, and Sensex and Nifty had one of their best months of the year.

So does US data matter for Dalal Street or not? It does, quite a lot. It just doesn't work like a light switch where bad US news means India falls. Let me show you how it actually works, and then what really happened this month.

## The 4 Pipes: how a number in Washington reaches your demat account

### Pipe 1: Foreign money

This is the big one. Foreign institutional investors (FIIs) own a large chunk of India's listed shares, so when they move, the index moves.

Put yourself in the shoes of a fund manager in New York. The US government will pay you close to 5% a year on a 10-year bond, with almost no risk. For India to be worth the trouble, with currency risk, distance and valuations to worry about, it has to promise a lot more than that. Every time US yields climb, that sum gets harder for India. When yields fall, money goes looking for growth again, and India is usually near the top of the list.

### Pipe 2: The rupee

Higher US rates usually make the dollar stronger, and a stronger dollar usually means a weaker rupee. That matters beyond the stock market. India imports most of its crude oil and pays in dollars, so a weak rupee eventually shows up in petrol prices and inflation at home.

### Pipe 3: Sectors react differently

Not everything falls together. IT companies like TCS and Infosys earn a big share of revenue from US clients, in dollars. A strong US economy keeps those clients spending, and a weaker rupee makes each dollar worth more when converted. Banks and financials tend to move with foreign flows and rate expectations. Metals follow global growth and the dollar, since commodities are priced in dollars.

### Pipe 4: The RBI's elbow room

If the Fed is raising rates while the RBI wants to hold or cut, the gap between US and Indian rates shrinks. Squeeze that gap too far and the rupee comes under pressure. So the Fed quietly limits how freely the RBI can move, and that's the route by which US policy can eventually reach your loan EMI.

None of this is a law of physics. Company earnings, valuations and money from Indian mutual fund SIPs often matter just as much. But these four pipes are where US data usually shows up first.

## What actually happened in November

### Foreign investors came roaring back

Despite all the hawkish US news, foreign investors bought **₹1,12,095.78 crore** of Indian equities in November, according to NSDL and NSE data reported by Business Today. Reports are calling it a record month.

To put that in context, FIIs had been selling India for most of 2026. Even after November, one estimate still has them as net sellers of around ₹1.73 lakh crore for the year. So this was a sharp U-turn, not a continuing trend.

### The market ran up, then took a breather

Sensex and Nifty gained 2.9% and 2.6% in the week to 20 November, and reports had both up around 10% for the month at the peak. On 23 November, Sensex fell 1,484 points as traders booked profits after a two-day rally.

### The rupee got stronger, not weaker

On 20 November, the rupee rose 0.62% to close at 92.71 per dollar, its strongest level in seven months. That's Pipe 1 feeding Pipe 2. To buy Indian shares, foreigners first have to buy rupees, and that demand pushes the currency up.

### And the sectors split the way the pipes predict

During the 23 November fall, Nifty Metal was the worst performer and banks lagged. Nifty IT went the other way and rose **2%**. Strong US growth is good news for companies that sell to US clients, even on a bad day for the wider market.

## So why didn't the US data hurt India more?

Three things were happening at once.

First, the hike was already expected. By mid-November, December hike odds were being quoted at around 85% to 96%. Markets don't react much to news everyone saw coming. They react to surprises.

Second, foreign funds had sold so much of India during the year that many were holding less than they wanted. When the mood turned, they had a lot of buying to do, and it was enough to outweigh worries about US rates.

Third, a US economy growing at 3.4% isn't all bad for India. It means demand for Indian exports and IT services.

But this balance can tip quickly. Analysts keep naming the same risks: US inflation, rising US bond yields, crude oil and geopolitics. If any of these turns sharply, the money that came in this month can leave just as fast.

## My take

US data matters to Indian investors far more than most of us give it credit for. It just works through foreign money, the rupee and a few specific sectors, not as a straight line from Washington to Sensex.

In November, a wave of foreign buying drowned out the bad news from the US. Whether that holds depends on two dates, 25 November and 9 December. If PCE comes in hot and the Fed sounds like it's planning more than one hike, I'd expect that foreign money to get nervous again.

**What would change my mind:** if the US 10-year yield breaks and holds above 5% *and* daily FII data turns to steady net selling for a week or more, I'd stop treating November's buying as the start of a trend and treat it as a one-month bounce.

## FAQ

**How does US inflation affect the Indian stock market?**
Mainly through foreign investors. Higher US inflation pushes up US interest rates and bond yields, which makes safe US assets more attractive than Indian shares. That can lead to FII selling, a weaker rupee, and pressure on Sensex and Nifty. The effect is strongest when the US data is a surprise.

**Why did Sensex and Nifty rise in November 2026 despite the US data?**
Foreign investors bought a reported ₹1,12,095.78 crore of Indian equities in November after selling for most of the year. A December Fed hike was already largely expected, so the US data didn't come as a shock.

**Which Indian sectors are most affected by US economic data?**
IT stocks are the most directly linked, because companies like TCS and Infosys earn much of their revenue from US clients. Banks are sensitive to foreign flows and interest rates. Metals react to global growth and the dollar.

**How does a Fed rate hike affect the rupee?**
A Fed hike usually strengthens the dollar and can pull foreign money out of India, both of which tend to weaken the rupee. In November 2026 the opposite happened for a while: strong foreign buying pushed the rupee to 92.71, a seven-month high.

**When is the next US data that could move Indian markets?**
The US releases Q3 GDP (second estimate) and October PCE inflation on 25 November 2026 at 7:00 PM IST. Then the November jobs report on 4 December, and the Fed's rate decision on 9 December.
